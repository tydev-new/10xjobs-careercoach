# Storybank — patterns that get you there faster

Guides, not rules. The goals decide when you are done; these are what
tends to work on the way. Read this before a capture session, and when a
loop is stuck.

## Asking

**Ask ONE prompt at a time. Wait.** A numbered menu of questions is a
form, and an intake that feels like a form gets form-shaped answers.

**Aim before you phrase.** Pick the target competency from
`storybank.md § Coverage` first, then choose a prompt that points there.
An interview-style question in the target role's own vocabulary — *"walk
me through a deployment that was failing"* — usually beats the generic
form once the candidate is warmed up.

**Aim at a story you already know exists.** `profile.md`'s story seeds
were captured for this. *"Your profile mentions the no-show dashboard —
walk me through it"* gets further than *"tell me about a time you showed
initiative."*

**Reflective prompts** are the opener, and the fallback when targeting
feels forced:

- *Peak* — "Think about a time you were at your best at work. What were
  you doing? What made it different from a typical day?" · "When have you
  felt most energised by your work?"
- *Challenge* — "What's the hardest professional situation you've
  navigated? What made it hard specifically?" · "Tell me about a time you
  changed your mind about something important."
- *Impact* — "When have you made something happen that wouldn't have
  happened without you?" · "Tell me about a time you convinced someone
  who disagreed with you."
- *Failure* — "What's a decision you'd make differently with hindsight?"
  · "When have things not gone according to plan?"

**Ask for the number once, plainly, then move on.** `TODO: <what to get>`
costs nothing; a stalled conversation costs the session.

**Capture at the first sign of a story, then drill.** Writing early and
refining the file beats holding it in the conversation until it is
perfect — and an interrupted session keeps the story.

## Shaping — what makes a story land

From Matthew Dicks, *Storyworthy*:

- **Every story is a moment of transformation** — "I used to think X,
  then Y happened, and now I know Z." Find what changed in their
  understanding; that gap is what makes it memorable.
- **Stories need stakes.** What was at risk? Without stakes it is an
  anecdote.
- **Start as close to the end as possible.** The most common revision is
  "you started in the wrong place" — no 90 seconds of scene-setting.
- **The But & Therefore test.** Replace "and then" with "but" or
  "therefore". If you can't, the story has no tension.
- **One vivid scene anchors it** — the sentence someone said, the page of
  red failure logs, the hand-soldered board. That is what the interviewer
  remembers.

## Drawing out the earned secret

Ask against the captured story:

1. "What did you believe before that turned out to be wrong?"
2. "What would surprise people who haven't done this?"
3. "What do most people in your field get wrong about this?"
4. "What counterintuitive lesson did you learn?"
5. "What would you tell your past self?"

**It qualifies** when it comes from direct experience, isn't obvious,
is something most people in the field get wrong or miss, is backed by a
specific story or metric, and survives being challenged.

**It doesn't qualify** as generic advice ("communication is important"),
book wisdom ("psychological safety matters"), an obvious observation, an
unevidenced prediction, or a borrowed podcast insight.

## Improving by score band

Diagnose by where the story is, not by generic advice:

- **1–2 — missing raw material.** "What's missing that you remember but
  haven't included?" · "What was actually hard about this?" Candidates
  often strip the tension out.
- **3 — good bones, no proof.** Target quantified impact, alternatives
  considered, or the earned secret. "What numbers could you attach, even
  rough ones?" · "What other approaches did you consider?"
- **4 — strong but undifferentiated.** Target the earned secret and the
  transformation moment. "What do you know from this that most people in
  your role wouldn't?"

## Red-teaming a story

Five lenses, run together:

1. **Assumption audit** — what must be true for this to land?
2. **Blind-spot scan** — what is invisible to them about their own story?
3. **Pre-mortem** — how does it fail live? Where does it lose attention?
4. **Devil's advocate** — where does a sceptic attack? Which follow-ups
   expose weakness?
5. **Strengthening path** — the one change that makes it airtight.

## Answering when the story doesn't exist

Four patterns, prescribed by name and by when each applies:

1. **Adjacent Bridge** — *"closest I've come is [adjacent experience];
   here's what transfers…"* Use when a strength-2 story or an adjacent
   experience exists.
2. **Hypothetical with Self-Awareness** — *"I haven't done this,
   honestly. Here's how I'd approach it and what I'd want to learn
   fast."* No story, no adjacent experience.
3. **Reframe to Strength** — *"not my strongest area; here's what I bring
   that addresses the same need."* A strength-1 story exists — don't
   deliver it.
4. **Growth Narrative** — *"an identified growth area; here's what I've
   already started doing."* A known development area.

## The three gaps always worth checking

- **feedback**, given and received — show caring personally *and*
  challenging directly; never present avoiding hard feedback as kindness
- **conflict or a difficult colleague** — the strongest answers show they
  understood the other side, not just their own frustration
- **a true failure** — a bank of all wins and saves reads as evasive

## Finding themes when the candidate can't

Bottom-up (Donna Lichaw): a childhood peak experience, a recent work
peak, and the meandering path into the current role, laid on top of each
other — superpowers show up at the intersections. Related: the
Kryptonite reframe — ask *"how is this weakness serving you?"* before
trying to eliminate it.

## Running a find-gaps pass

Read `storybank.md § Coverage`. Check Primary AND Secondary skills
against it — a Secondary match is workable coverage, never full. A draft
covering a competency is named as a draft, not counted.

Rank each gap: **Critical** (will be tested, no story even as secondary)
· **Important** (likely, only weak or secondary coverage) ·
**Nice-to-have**. For critical gaps, ask whether an existing story can be
reframed or a new experience is genuinely needed.

## Running a retrieval drill

The bank isn't real until it works under pressure; the moment of truth is
finding the right story in three seconds mid-interview.

10 questions, 10 seconds each, answered with **story ID + opening line
only**. Mix behavioural / situational / curveball, and include 1–2 with
no good fit to test gap-handling instinct. Score retrieval speed (<3s
strong, >7s needs work), match quality, opening line.

Debrief hesitations (retrieval gaps), wrong reaches (indexing issues),
and no-fit questions (they feed gap analysis). Progression: standard →
role-specific → curveball → 5-second rounds.

## Naming the narrative identity

Read every story's full text, secret, and deploy case. Cluster by
**underlying theme**, not surface skill — "building systems where none
existed", not "leadership". If a theme could describe a generic
candidate, go deeper.

Name 2–3 dominant themes and the **sharpest edge**: the theme hardest to
replicate and most likely to be remembered. Flag **orphan stories** (no
theme — dilution or retirement candidates) and **fragile themes** (one
story is an anecdote; two is a pattern).

Output the themes with their story IDs, the sharpest edge and how many
stories leverage it, coverage counts, and how to use the themes in
answers, in questions-to-ask, and in positioning.

## Antipatterns

- A numbered menu of questions.
- Sharpening a vague answer into a specific one so the file looks
  finished.
- Marking a story `draft` because it is incomplete. Those are different
  things (`eval.md`).
- Narrating file operations — *"Now the story file:"* — instead of saying
  what you learned.

## Proposing a new pattern

If a round shows something works, say so with the evidence from
`storybank-history.md` and let the candidate or the builder decide. A
pattern is never self-adopted, and a proposed one lands in the
candidate's workspace, not in this file.
